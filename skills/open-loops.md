# Open loops

> Everything you're waiting on someone else for, written down with a date, so you stop carrying it in your head. · **Payoff:** Peace of mind · **Setup:** about 15 minutes, once

A loop is anything where you've done your part and the ball is in someone else's court. The plumber who was going to call back. The friend who said they'd send dates. The invoice. The reply you're owed. Individually they're nothing. Together they're a low hum of *am I forgetting something* that follows you around all week.

**Why it's worth it.** The cost of an open loop isn't the task, it's the remembering. Your head keeps a background process running on every unresolved thing, and it runs whether or not there's anything you can do about it. Writing a loop down with a date turns "don't forget this" into "not today," which is the entire relief. You are not more organised afterwards. You are just carrying less.

**The setup (once).**
1. Make a list — a note, a spreadsheet, a table, whatever you'll actually open. Four columns: **who**, **what you're waiting for**, **when it started**, **when to check back**.
2. Empty your head into it. Fifteen minutes, everything you can think of that's sitting with someone else.
3. Give every row a check-back date. Two business days for a quick answer, a week for a favour, a month for something that isn't really urgent. The date is the whole point — it's what lets you close the tab in your head.
4. Look at the list once a week. Anything past its date gets one nudge or gets deleted. Nothing else needs doing.

**Where AI earns its place (and where it doesn't).** The tedious part is finding the loops you've forgotten — and they're mostly sitting in your sent mail. "Show me threads where I sent the last message more than a week ago and nobody replied" is a genuinely good use of an assistant with inbox access, and it will surface things you'd lost entirely. Drafting the nudge is fair game too; a three-line follow-up is exactly the kind of blank page worth outsourcing.

Where it doesn't belong: **deciding whether to chase at all.** Some silences should be left alone. If you've told someone to come back with a number, following up to ask whether they're going to costs you the leverage you just built. If a thread touches something you haven't decided yet — a job, a rate, a favour you're not sure you want — chasing it forces a decision on someone else's schedule. An assistant will cheerfully draft that email because the date came up. The date is not a reason. Keep the judgement, hand over the sweeping.

**Keep it small.** On a bad week: open the list, look at the single oldest thing, and either send one line or delete the row. Deleting counts. A loop you've decided to drop is closed, and closed is the goal — not chased.

## Notes from running one
- **Write down why you're waiting, not just that you are.** "Sent quote, waiting" tells you nothing in three weeks. "Sent quote 12th, said he'd have a number by month end" tells you whether it's late.
- **A silence is not evidence about a person.** Most quiet threads are somebody's full inbox, not a signal. Loops track the ball, not the relationship — that's what [Keep in Touch](keep-in-touch.md) is for, and mixing them makes both worse.
- **Let rows die.** A list that only grows becomes another thing to avoid. Anything you've stopped caring about gets deleted without ceremony, and the deleting is a feature.
